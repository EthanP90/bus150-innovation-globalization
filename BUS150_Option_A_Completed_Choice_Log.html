Our recommendation

Launch Module Robotics through a Mexican shelter provider, assemble functional modules with trained people, and sell directly to US school robotics teams. Keep design and customer support in South Florida and hold a modest US stock of finished modules. Mexico supplies the cost advantage; modular design, reliable testing, and available spare parts supply the customer value.

Shock round (stop 7) — simulated example

Hypothetical card: A new, non-exempt 20% import charge applies to our Mexican modules. Assume a customs value of $100 per module. This is a classroom stress test, not a statement of the current tariff rate.

Did our switch signal fire? Yes. The added charge is 20% × $100 = $20. Mexico’s modeled variable delivered cost rises from $112 to $132. At a $175 net selling price, contribution falls from $63 (36.0%) to $43 (24.6%), below our 30% floor. The Florida comparison remains $129 only under the scenario assumption that its input costs are unchanged.

Decision: hedge while retaining Option A as the primary path. Freeze new long-term Mexican capacity commitments, check the charge’s actual scope, and qualify a small Florida backup batch. Continue fulfilling existing orders from US inventory. Do not immediately abandon trained staff and tooling for an unproven replacement line.

What does that cost us? Assume $3,000 to qualify the backup and $140 per module for a 50-module Florida pilot. Compared with producing those 50 units in Mexico at the shocked $132 cost, the pilot adds 50 × ($140 − $132) = $400. Total incremental hedge cost is $3,400, before any additional inventory. The $140 pilot estimate is higher than the $129 Florida steady-state model because small trial batches lack scale.

Next decision: If the shock lasts three months, compare a full transfer using updated quotes. At 200 modules/month, the steady-state model gives Mexico $3,600/month and Florida $5,200/month after modeled fixed operating costs. A hypothetical $12,000 incremental transfer cost would take $12,000 ÷ $1,600 = 7.5 months to recover, excluding disruption and working capital. Switch only if forecast demand and the expected shock duration justify it; otherwise retain flexible capacity and revise pricing or inputs.

Looking back

Did we switch paths? Our planned launch stays on Option A across all eight stops. In the simulated shock, we add a limited domestic backup rather than immediately making a complete transfer. This distinguishes committing to a strategy from refusing to change when its assumptions fail.

What changes after choosing our first customer? Stop 8 makes us revise stop 5: direct cross-border delivery to each school is too dependent on border timing. Instead, consolidate truck shipments into US stock before competition season. Begin with two weeks of forecast demand—100 modules at the assumed 200 modules/month, using four weeks per month. Assume $7 of the $12 freight allowance brings each module to US stock and $5 covers onward delivery. Cost to reach stock is then $80 + $9 + $8 + $7 = $104 per module, so 100 modules tie up about $10,400, excluding the $3 expected rework allowance. This freight split is another planning assumption; verify actual cash requirements and payment terms. Build stock only for confirmed/credible demand, track it by module, and do not promise next-day delivery nationwide.

Innovation and globalization work together. Globalization determines where we source, assemble, and sell. Innovation determines the value of compatible modules, repeatable testing, the shelter arrangement, and a responsive delivery process. A low manufacturing wage is useful only if the complete system delivers reliable modules at an affordable price.

Interpretation: Mexico saves $17 in variable delivered cost per module, or $3,400 at 200 units. After $1,000 of additional monthly fixed cost, its modeled advantage is $2,400 per month. At only 50 units, however, Mexico loses $1,850/month and Florida loses $1,700/month. The location choice depends on volume as well as wages. Mexico’s monthly result exceeds Florida’s only above roughly 59 units because $1,000 ÷ $17 ≈ 58.8.

What is included and excluded: Fixed estimates include site/service overhead, shelter administration where relevant, and base US support. Variable freight is an end-to-end average; the US stock point uses our base rather than a separately leased warehouse. The figures exclude startup/tooling outlays, financing, taxes, founder salaries, depreciation, and inventory carrying cost. The monthly result is therefore a planning contribution after listed fixed costs, not net profit. No additional product tariff is included in the baseline; that is a model assumption, not an asserted exemption. Actual charges on inputs and modules must be incorporated into quotes before committing.

Two sensitivities: If Mexican labor rises 20%, assembly cost becomes $10.80 and total variable cost $113.80; contribution remains $61.20 (35.0%). If a hypothetical 10% charge applies to a $100 customs value, cost becomes $122 and contribution $53 (30.3%); the $7 advantage over Florida fires stop 1’s cost-review signal if it persists for two months. This shows that trade exposure can matter more than a modest wage increase. Do not add separate scenarios together unless modeling their combined effect explicitly.

How the team can test the decision without groupthink

This is a proposed review process, not a record of a meeting. Before signing a supplier contract, each of Ethan, Kell, Cole, Estefany, and Sebastian should independently rank the options and write one reason the preferred Mexico path could fail. Rotate a challenger role, compare the same cost/quality/delivery assumptions, and record any unresolved disagreement. Test a small batch against the 95% on-time and 2% defect limits before increasing orders. A unanimous preference is useful only when contrary evidence has been considered.

For each monthly review, record actual delivered cost by module, first-pass quality, customer delivery performance, cash tied up in stock, and repeat orders. Compare these with the explicit switch signals above. Change the decision when the evidence changes, rather than defending an earlier vote.
